SCHOOL BUSDRIVER BACKGROUND (verbatim detail)
The 54-year-old school busdriver had been hired by the TJA School District on August 1, 1983,
as a secretary and bookkeeper. Between April 1986 and July 1989, she served as a bus dispatcher.
She passed the Illinois written school busdriver examination on January 2, 1987, and took the
school bus road test three times between January 2 and 5, 1987, before passing it. She was
selected on July 15, 1989, for the position of assistant transportation director, responsible for
auditing and evaluating the performance of 92 school busdrivers, though she was not herself
certified as a trainer. On the day of the accident, the regular busdriver for this route was ill, and
the substitute (the accident driver) had never driven this particular route before, either in a car or
a school bus. She had completed all but one stop on the route as the bus approached the grade
crossing.

RAILROAD SIGNAL WARNING TIME -- THUMBWHEEL HISTORY (verbatim technical detail)
The desired advance warning time for the crossing signal is entered via a "thumbwheel" switch on
the microprocessor. During the 5 years before the accident, the thumbwheel had been set at 30
seconds for the northernmost railroad track, with the narrow band shunt connected to the rails
approximately 3,053 feet from the edge of the crossing. On October 11, 1995 -- two weeks before
the accident -- Union Pacific Railroad reset the thumbwheel to 25 seconds "to ensure consistency
at that crossing and throughout the railroad's property." UP officials stated the change did not
alter the minimum constant warning time of 20 seconds. A UP representative testified at the
Safety Board's public hearing in January 1996: "We design our circuits for 25 seconds to give a
5-second buffer... because there are conditions out on the track that make us unable to give an
exact warning time... So we put in a 5-second buffer to this 20 seconds."

DOCUMENTATION AND COMMUNICATION BETWEEN IDOT AND THE RAILROAD
On August 4, 1987, the C&NW railroad sent IDOT design plans indicating the railroad grade
crossing approach circuit lengths were "3,080 feet, 30 seconds at 70 mph." At that time no
highway traffic signals existed at Algonquin Road. In April 1991, C&NW submitted construction
prints to IDOT indicating an approach warning time for track #1 (southern track) of 25 seconds
and 30 seconds for track #2 (northern track). On May 11, 1992, C&NW provided IDOT with a
"project completion report" that answered "yes" to the question "Do all approach start distances
provide a minimum operation of 20 seconds before arrival of any train?"

At the Safety Board's public hearing in January 1996, UP presented construction prints dated July
14, 1995, indicating a minimum of 20 seconds warning time. IDOT representatives testified they
had never seen these construction prints before the hearing, and that they believed based on other
documents they had a warning time of 25 to 30 seconds from the railroad signal system. When
asked how much information about warning time the railroad provided when a signal is turned
on, the IDOT engineering technician responded: "You don't get any written documents... if we
inquired, they [the railroad] could give us an answer... We would establish some timing and put
in some test calls and we could observe how long it was from the time we received the call from
the railroad until their gates were horizontal and adjust our timing on that basis."

WARNING TIME TESTS -- RESULTS (verbatim statistics)
On October 25-26, 1995, Safety Board investigators timed approximately 15 trains and found
warning times ranging from about 20 seconds (shortest) to 155 seconds (longest). On October 29,
1995, investigators conducted tests replicating the accident train's movements and recorded
warning times of 20.73 and 21.12 seconds. In November 1995, IDOT's independent postaccident
testing found a 22.7-second average warning time for 70-mph eastbound trains (with some as low
as 21 seconds) and a 26.8-second average for 70-mph westbound trains. Critically, in 34 out of 66
tests, the traffic lights provided 10 seconds or less for traffic on Algonquin Road to clear the
railroad tracks after the highway signal's preemption sequence began.

HIGHWAY SIGNAL PREEMPTION SEQUENCE HISTORY
Between January 1990 and October 1994, IDOT was unable to coordinate the traffic phases of
three adjacent intersections (US 14 at Lincoln Avenue, Algonquin Road, and State Route 22)
through a master controller, so they operated in an uncoordinated mode. During this period, if a
train approached while US 14 had a green indication, the sequence allowed only 6 seconds to
elapse before a green indication was displayed for northbound Algonquin Road (18 seconds if a
pedestrian button had been pushed). In October 1994, new signal controllers and a new master
controller were installed, enabling coordinated mode; the programmed sequence then included an
automatic 12-second pedestrian clearance phase, allowing 18 seconds before green for
northbound Algonquin Road. Testimony from IDOT's contractors indicated the preemption
sequence could take as long as 21 seconds to reach a green indication under certain
circumstances (e.g., if the preemption occurred within 3 seconds of US 14 receiving a green
indication, a further 3-second delay was programmed in to prevent motorists from receiving a
momentary green).

RESEARCH ON WARNING TIME PERCEPTION (verbatim citation)
A February 1991 FHWA-funded report by the University of Tennessee assessed the actions of
3,500 motorists during 445 train crossings at three active grade crossings. It found that extremely
short warning times "leave little margin of safety and poorly accommodate larger vehicles such
as combination trucks and buses," while warning times exceeding 40 seconds (flashing lights) or
60 seconds (gated crossings) can cause drivers to lose confidence in the traffic control system.
Most motorists expect a train to arrive within 20 seconds of the traffic control device activating;
warning times in excess of 30 to 40 seconds caused many motorists to engage in risky crossing
behavior.

ACTIVATION OF CROSSING WARNING DEVICES TESTS
At the request of the Safety Board, Safety Engineering Associates (an engineering consultant
retained by Union Pacific) performed six tests to determine the delay between circuit activation
and the actual activation of lights and bell. Light circuit tests ranged from 803 to 819
milliseconds; bell circuit tests ranged from 800 to 802 milliseconds. The average delay time
across all six tests was 805 milliseconds (8/10 of a second).
