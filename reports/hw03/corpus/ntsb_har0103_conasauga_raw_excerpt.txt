BUS VIDEO RECORDER FINDINGS (verbatim detail)
All Murray County School District buses are equipped with a video monitoring system (a Silent
Witness brand VCR and color video camera). The camera was mounted at the front and center of
the bus, pointed down the passenger compartment; the driver and first two rows were not within
the camera's line of sight. District policy required a separate videocassette for each day of the
week. Investigators examined the videocassette from the VCR plus 14 videocassette reels and 7
segments of loose magnetic tape recovered separately at the scene. Using landmarks and sound
recordings, staff confirmed 12 instances of the bus crossing the railroad tracks on tape, in every
instance with the accident driver at the wheel. The bus did NOT stop before crossing on nine of
those occasions, including the day of the accident -- eight of the nine were in the two weeks
immediately preceding the accident. It stopped only twice, both times when another vehicle was
present behind it.

REPORTING NEAR MISSES AT GRADE CROSSINGS
After the accident, another locomotive engineer reported that 11 days earlier (March 17, 2000,
between 3:00 and 3:30 p.m.), he had seen a school bus cross the Liberty Church Road tracks
without stopping, passing over the crossing about 500 feet ahead of his train. He did not report
the incident at the time because he could not read the identifying numbers on the back of the bus.
CSXT locomotives commonly carry a placard depicting a school bus stopped at a grade crossing
while a train approaches, instructing crews to report near misses; CSXT operating rule 100-F
requires crews to report such occurrences, including failures of school bus drivers to stop or
observe reasonable precautions, to the CSXT Police Communications Center.

1998 SAFETY STUDY ON PASSIVE GRADE CROSSINGS (verbatim findings)
The Safety Board's 1998 study (NTSB/SS-98/02) investigated 60 accidents at passive grade
crossings between December 1995 and August 1996 and cited driver error as the primary cause in
49 of 60 cases. The study concluded that consolidation (separation and closure) of passive
crossings is the most effective way to eliminate vehicle-train accidents; the next most desirable
method is equipping crossings with active warning devices; and installation/enforcement of stop
signs at passive crossings would provide consistent information to motorists. The study made 19
recommendations, including H-98-28 to the U.S. DOT (full funding within 3 years for stop/stop-
ahead signs at passive crossings) and H-98-34 to the States (install stop signs within 2 years of
receiving federal funding, unless a traffic engineering analysis determines this would reduce
safety, in which case upgrade to active devices or eliminate the crossing).

Of the 11 passive crossings in Polk County and 8 in Murray County examined by investigators,
only 3 in Murray County were equipped with stop signs; none in Polk County had any. As of the
report's writing, Recommendation H-98-34 was classified "Open-Acceptable Response" for
Tennessee and 16 other States, "Open-Unacceptable Response" for 7 States, "Closed-
Unacceptable Action" for 4 States, and "Closed-Acceptable Action" for only 2 States (Idaho and
Hawaii) -- Idaho because it already required stop signs at all passive crossings by law, and Hawaii
because it had only two passive crossings, both already equipped.

DOT HIGHWAY-RAIL CROSSING INVENTORY -- DATA QUALITY ISSUES (verbatim finding)
Investigators examined the FRA's Highway Rail Crossing Inventory database entries for 10
crossings in the Liberty Church Road vicinity and found significant discrepancies: the inventory
listed the track speed as 10 mph less than actual speed in seven instances, and did not list track
speed at all in one instance. The inventory listed 13 trains per day for five crossings and 16 trains
per day for four crossings, while CSXT actually reported 30 to 35 trains per day traveling over
each crossing -- roughly double to triple what the federal inventory showed.

IN-VEHICLE WARNING SYSTEMS -- FIELD TESTS (verbatim detail)
In 1997, the Minnesota Department of Transportation tested a three-point in-vehicle warning
system in a school district where 30 equipped buses each crossed two to five grade crossings per
day. Surveyed drivers: 80 percent thought the system provided valuable warning information; 15
percent said it affected their driving behavior; one respondent said it helped her avoid a crash. In
2000, the Illinois Department of Transportation's Intelligent Transportation Systems Program
Office piloted a similar system near Chicago with 300 test vehicles (school buses, emergency
vehicles, commercial vehicles) at five active grade crossings. Preliminary results indicated
drivers liked the additional warnings but would have preferred earlier alerts, before lights and
gates activated. The in-vehicle receiver device was projected to cost about $100 per vehicle; the
wayside transmitters cost $20,000-$30,000 each at the time, with costs expected to fall as
production scaled.

FOUR SAFETY INITIATIVES PROPOSED TO TENNESSEE (verbatim, October 16, 2000 letter)
Following this accident, the NTSB Chairman wrote to the Governor of Tennessee suggesting four
initiatives: (1) install warning devices at all passive grade crossings traversed daily by school
buses; (2) strengthen criteria for installing active warning devices by including the frequency of
school bus crossings as a factor; (3) strengthen Tennessee's CDL manual and exam to include
questions on passive grade crossing safety; and (4) install noise-reducing "option kill switches" in
school buses (already being piloted in Florida and Kentucky) so drivers can turn off radios and
fans when approaching a crossing.
