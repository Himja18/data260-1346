PREFACE / ORIGIN
This document contains recommended guidelines to assist rail transit systems in assessing,
developing, documenting and improving their capability for responding to emergency situations,
and to coordinate these efforts with outside emergency response organizations. It was developed
in response to recommendations made by the National Transportation Safety Board's hearing
concerning rail transit system safety, with cooperation from the American Public Transit
Association (APTA) and representatives from various transit systems and emergency response
organizations.

1. INTRODUCTION
Safety planning has two basic phases: a preventive phase (preventing the occurrence of an
incident) and a reactive phase (responding once an incident has occurred, minimizing its effect).
These guidelines address the reactive phase. They cover four primary elements: Emergency Plan
Development, Training, Facilities and Equipment, and Vehicles. The Facilities/Equipment and
Vehicles sections are intended primarily for planning new systems, system extensions, and
system rehabilitation -- not expected to have a major impact on existing facilities.

2. EMERGENCY PLAN DEVELOPMENT
An Emergency Plan should contain: statement of policy, definition of scope, agreements with
emergency response organizations, transit system functions and responsibilities, emergency
procedures, general response capability criteria, and supporting documentation.

2.2 SCOPE - procedures should be developed for: reporting the emergency; evaluating and
establishing its parameters; notifying emergency response personnel; dispatching personnel and
equipment; coordinating activities of all emergency response personnel; protecting passengers,
personnel and equipment; evacuation; keeping stakeholders informed; and restoring normal
operations.

2.3 INTER-ORGANIZATIONAL AGREEMENTS - Rail transit systems should establish
agreements with outside organizations (fire departments, EMS, police) prior to emergencies.
Agreements should include at minimum: a list of participating organizations with contact
information (24-hour coverage); an outline of personnel/equipment to be provided; financial
responsibilities; jurisdictional boundaries; how the document was developed/approved; how
changes will be reviewed; a definitions section; and identification of training responsibilities.

2.5 EMERGENCY PROCEDURES should address at minimum: train fires, trainway fires, station
fires, train derailment/collision, death or injury to a patron, train evacuation, bomb threat, total
power failure, flammable/combustible liquid or vapor intrusion, trainway intrusion,
suicide/trainway injuries or fatalities, and natural disasters (high winds, flood, earthquake).

2.6 GENERAL RESPONSE CAPABILITY CRITERIA - key response areas: notification,
communications, removal of traction power from vehicles, ventilation, coupling/uncoupling of
vehicles, evacuation, and firefighting. A suggested decision-checklist includes questions such as:
Who provides the initial report? Who decides the reported incident is in fact an emergency and
determines its category? If reported while a train is moving between stations, who decides
whether to stop the train or move it to the next station? Who decides to turn off traction power,
and how is it verified to be off? Who decides to evacuate a station or train, and what criteria are
used?

2.7 EMERGENCY PLAN SUPPORTING DOCUMENTATION (worked examples cited in the
original document): Inter-Organizational Emergency Procedures such as "NYCTA Executive
Interagency Standard Operating Procedure," BART's "Emergency Plan," and WMATA METRO's
"Rapid Rail Transit Fire/Rescue Operations Procedure Guidelines." Decision-Making Aids for
Central Controllers cited include CTA's Milwaukee-Kimball and State Street Subway Maps,
WMATA's "Fire Maps," PATH's carousel of slides in the Central Control console, and BART's
Microcomputer Data Retrieval System. Major Accident Investigation reports cited as inputs
include the "PATH Investigation Report, March 16, 1982 Fire" and the NTSB's "BART Fire on
Train #117 and Evacuation of Passengers While in Transbay Tube, San Francisco, January 17,
1979."

3. TRAINING
Three training phases recommended: initial operational and emergency response training for all
employees; specialized emergency response training for certain groups depending on job
requirements; and refresher/retraining courses for both phases.

3.1.2 SPECIALIZED EMERGENCY TRAINING - Operating/supervisory personnel, maintenance
personnel, and transit police should be taught appropriate responses to: fire/smoke in tunnel;
fire/smoke in or under train; fire/smoke on or adjacent to trainway; fire/smoke at station;
derailment, collision, structural collapse; passenger evacuation from train; person on trainway;
sick passenger; hostage situation; civil disorder; bomb threat; total power failure; flood;
flammable/combustible liquid/vapor intrusion; severe weather/natural disasters; and combined-
category situations (e.g., derailment + fire + evacuation together).

Specialized training areas detailed further: emergency tunnel evacuation, emergency tunnel
ventilation, firefighting (transit personnel's first-priority role is assisting passenger evacuation and
helping fire department access -- actual firefighting is primarily the fire department's job, but
basic firefighting training builds confidence and enables fighting small fires before the fire
department arrives when prudent), passenger emergency care (first aid, CPR, treatment of head/
back injuries, respiratory blockages), crowd control and panic prevention, emergency uncoupling
of rail cars, and removal of traction power.

3.1.4.3 EMERGENCY RESPONSE TRAINING DRILLS - Recommended drill types: drills prior
to system start-up (on unopened new sections), drills during non-revenue service periods, and
drills during revenue service (posing a hypothetical emergency without actually dispatching real
fire/rescue personnel or interrupting service) -- these can be general notice, employee notice, or
no-notice drills. Monitor locations should allow observation of: initial communications between
train operator and Central Control; implementation of evacuation procedures; the passenger
evacuation process itself; EMT first aid in the triage area; use of evacuation equipment; emergency
command post operations; and firefighters' response efforts. A post-drill simulation critique
(often video-recorded) should review monitor reports and assess remedial action needs.

3.3 PUBLIC EDUCATION - Passenger emergency preparedness training should cover: how to
recognize and immediately report emergencies to transit employees; how to recognize system
hazards (third rail, high voltage equipment, passage between cars); how to recognize and operate
emergency-related equipment (vehicle intercom, fire extinguishers, on-board emergency stop
devices, side/end doors, ladders/ramps/planks, subway emergency exits, station emergency
phones); and how to anticipate special needs of fellow passengers (children, elderly, handicapped)
during emergencies. A separate School Safety Program is recommended to teach children in
communities adjoining rail transit trainway about hazards like third-rail power and the dangers of
throwing rocks, tampering with switches, or placing objects on tracks -- especially important
before a new system or extension opens.

4. FACILITIES AND EQUIPMENT
Covers passenger stations, trainway, and Central Control, addressing construction, lighting,
access/egress, communications, ventilation, fire protection support, intrusion protection, traction
power removal, graphics, and emergency power.

4.1.3 ACCESS/EGRESS (stations) - guidance includes: each emergency exit should be identified
by graphics and a distinctive-colored light with dual light circuitry; emergency exit doors should
never be locked on the inside; exits should not open onto hazardous areas (vehicle roadways,
parking areas) and should be kept clear of obstructions; exits should be able to be readily opened
from outside by fire department/rescue personnel; vertical ladders in emergency exits should be
prohibited; and non-egress doors/ladders should be clearly labeled "Not an Exit."

4.1.9 TRACTION POWER (stations) - a means for locally removing power from specific third-rail
or catenary sections should exist (e.g., local emergency trip stations), and Central Control should
also be able to remove power from specific sections remotely; devices for verifying power has
actually been removed should be incorporated.

4.2.3 ACCESS/EGRESS (trainway) - underground trainways built by cut-and-cover typically have
shallow depth enabling vent shafts and emergency exit stairways to the surface; deep bore tunnels
and underwater tubes have limited direct-to-surface exit capability and instead use cross passages,
fire doors to the opposite trackway, or a separate center passageway. Elevated trainways have
rarely had emergency exits; the preferred evacuation method is moving the train (or part of it) to
the nearest station or bringing a rescue train alongside for passenger transfer -- as a last resort,
fire department ladder trucks have been used, described as "an extremely slow and time-
consuming process." At-grade/open-cut trainways typically use emergency access gates openable
by transit personnel or rescue crews, without keys from the inside; fire department personnel
should have keys or other means to open them from outside.

4.2.8 INTRUSION ALARM - Where rail transit tracks run adjacent to highways or share corridors
with freight/passenger railroads, motor vehicle accidents or train derailments could intrude onto
the transit track area; both train operators and Central Control must be immediately alerted to
such intrusions, via physical barriers and/or a detection and alarm system.

5. VEHICLES
5.1.3.1 END DOORS - An emergency door opening control requiring no electrical power should
be located on the vehicle exterior; passengers should be able to move from car to car in an
emergency; locking of end doors is discouraged unless an interior emergency release exists.
5.1.3.2 SIDE DOORS - An interlocking door control in each operator cab should permit opening
all doors on either side of the train; an emergency door unlocking device requiring no electrical
power should exist both inside and outside the vehicle; side doors should be wide enough for two
people side by side.
5.1.7.1 EMERGENCY BRAKES - Each vehicle should have a fail-safe friction brake for manual
activation; emergency braking independent of the train operator should activate automatically if
any car in a train unintentionally uncouples.

APPENDIX B: EMERGENCY EQUIPMENT (representative list)
Air bag rescue and lifting system (capable of lifting/moving/shifting up to 146,000 pounds); axes;
bolt cutters; emergency evacuation cart; evacuation ladders and planks; fire extinguishers of
various types; fire hose and nozzles; first aid kits; flood lights; hand lights; hand tools; hydraulic
jacks and hydraulic pry/pinch/push/bend/tear/shear tools for extrication; insulating aprons, mats,
blankets, and gloves (electrical shock protection); jumpers/stingers for third rail power;
maintenance phone headsets; pry bars; radios; self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) with
spare air bottles; shoe lifts (to inhibit traction power collection on a vehicle); and stretchers
(Stokes baskets, Reeves stretchers).
