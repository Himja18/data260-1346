NTSB Organization, Mission, and Investigation Process - Reference Notes
Compiled from standard boilerplate language and organizational descriptions repeated across
multiple NTSB accident reports in this corpus.
Source: Consolidated from NTSB.gov accident report standard sections (see individual report
files for original document URLs).
Accessed: 2026-09-19

WHAT THE NTSB IS
The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) is an independent federal agency, established
in 1967, dedicated to promoting aviation, railroad, highway, marine, pipeline, and hazardous
materials safety. Congress mandated the agency through the Independent Safety Board Act of
1974 to investigate transportation accidents, determine probable causes, issue safety
recommendations, study transportation safety issues, and evaluate the safety effectiveness of
government agencies involved in transportation. As of more recent reports in this corpus (2024
NJ Transit tree collision report), the NTSB's charge also explicitly extends to "commercial
space" alongside aviation, railroad, highway, marine, and pipeline modes, and the agency
additionally serves as the appellate authority for FAA and US Coast Guard certificate enforcement
actions and adjudicates FAA civil penalty appeals -- responsibilities not present in the agency's
earlier self-description from 1990s-era reports in this corpus, reflecting how the agency's mandate
has grown over time.

HOW THE NTSB PUBLISHES FINDINGS
The NTSB makes its actions and decisions public through several document types found across
this corpus:
- Accident Reports (e.g., "Highway/Railroad Accident Report," "Railroad Accident Report,"
  "Railroad Investigation Report," "Highway Accident Brief") -- these focus on a single accident
  and include factual investigation findings, analysis, conclusions, probable cause, and
  recommendations.
- Safety Recommendation Letters (e.g., the L'Enfant Plaza oversight letter, R-15-31/32) -- sent
  directly to a government agency, company, or other entity urging specific action, sometimes
  marked "Urgent."
- Safety Studies (e.g., the 1998 study on passive grade crossings cited within the Conasauga
  report, NTSB/SS-98/02) -- broader investigations addressing patterns across many similar
  accidents rather than a single event.
- Special Investigation Reports (e.g., the 2014 report on Roadway Worker Protection cited in the
  FTA bus accident investigation background research document, NTSB/SIR 14-03) -- issue-
  focused investigations spanning multiple incidents or a systemic safety topic.

WHAT AN NTSB INVESTIGATION DOES AND DOES NOT DO
A recurring, near-verbatim statement across NTSB reports in this corpus clarifies the legal
character of an investigation: "accident/incident investigations are fact-finding proceedings with
no formal issues and no adverse parties... and are not conducted for the purpose of determining
the rights or liabilities of any person" (49 CFR Section 831.4). The NTSB explicitly does not
assign fault or blame in the legal sense; assignment of fault or legal liability is stated to be "not
relevant" to the agency's statutory mission of improving transportation safety. Correspondingly,
federal statute (49 U.S.C. Section 1154(b)) prohibits admitting any part of an NTSB report into
evidence, or otherwise using it, in a civil action for damages arising from a matter mentioned in
the report. This means that while an NTSB report may describe a "probable cause" in plain
factual language (e.g., "the busdriver's failure to stop before traversing the... grade crossing"),
this determination is explicitly not a legal fault-finding and cannot itself be used in court to
establish liability.

REPORT NUMBERING CONVENTIONS OBSERVED ACROSS THIS CORPUS
NTSB report numbers generally follow a TYPE-YY/NN pattern, where TYPE indicates report
category and mode (e.g., HAR = Highway Accident Report, RAR = Railroad Accident Report,
RIR = Railroad Investigation Report, HAB = Highway Accident Brief, SIR = Special
Investigation Report, SS = Safety Study), YY is the two-digit year of adoption/issuance, and NN
is a sequential number within that year. For example, NTSB/HAR-96/02 (Fox River Grove) was
the second Highway Accident Report adopted in 1996; NTSB/RAR-10/02 (Fort Totten) was the
second Railroad Accident Report adopted in 2010. Note that the "issued" or "adopted" year in the
report number reflects when the NTSB finalized the report, which can be one to several years
after the accident date itself occurred (e.g., the 2021 NJ Transit tree-strike accident produced
report RIR-25-19, issued in 2025 -- a roughly one-year investigation-to-publication gap that is
fairly typical for non-"Urgent" reports in this corpus).

DOCKET AND SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIALS
Several reports in this corpus reference a public docket system (accessible via the NTSB's Case
Analysis and Reporting Online, or "CAROL," system) where additional supporting materials are
held beyond what appears in the published report itself -- including police reports, photographs,
driver and witness statements, prior crash history data, engineering reports, and detailed timing
data (e.g., traffic signal sequences). The NYC transit bus report (HAB-18/10) and the SEPTA
Spring Garden Station report (RIR-22/03) both explicitly direct readers to search a specific
NTSB accident ID number in this docket system for more detail than the summary report itself
contains.

ADDITIONAL NOTE: DAMAGE ESTIMATES AND EQUIPMENT AGE AS RECURRING FACTORS
Across the verified accident reports in this corpus, the age of the involved equipment recurs as
a contributing theme even when it is not cited as a formal probable cause. The Miami APM
train involved in the 2008 collision was built in 1980 (28 years old at the time of the accident,
already past its intended design life according to the original maintenance contractor's stated
reason for declining to renew its contract). The Chicago Transit Authority railcars involved in
the 2014 O'Hare collision were originally manufactured 1981-1987 and rebuilt 1999-2002 (27-33
years old at the time of the accident), and were not equipped with true event recorders -- a
technology gap the NTSB had been trying to close since a 1996 recommendation, nearly two
decades before the O'Hare accident occurred. The WMATA rail cars and track circuit equipment
involved in the 2009 Fort Totten collision similarly dated to the system's original 1970s
construction. Estimated property damage figures varied enormously across the corpus's reports:
$11,196,796 for the 2014 O'Hare collision (a single-train accident with no fatalities), reflecting
that severe non-fatal property-damage accidents can carry very large dollar figures relative to
some fatal accidents in this corpus, since damage estimates and fatality counts are independent
dimensions of accident severity that a retrieval system should not conflate when answering
questions that ask specifically about one or the other.
