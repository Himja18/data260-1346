ABSTRACT
As part of FTA's effort to promote continuous safety improvement in the public transit industry,
Effective Practices in Performing Transit Bus Accident Investigations were developed to provide
bus transit agencies leading transit industry practices for performing investigations. This
supporting document examines each Safety Management System (SMS) element. Recommended
practices are not intended to be prescriptive; each agency tailors its investigation processes to its
unique operating environment.

SMS FRAMEWORK
Title 49 CFR Part 673 (the Public Transportation Agency Safety Plan Final Rule, effective July
19, 2018) requires each transit agency to establish and implement an SMS scaled to its size, scope,
and complexity, including four elements: Safety Management Policy, Safety Risk Management
(SRM), Safety Assurance (SA), and Safety Promotion. SMS is defined as "a formal, top-down,
organization-wide approach to managing safety risk and assuring the effectiveness of the transit
agency's safety risk mitigation."

KEY ROLES
Accountable Executive: typically the agency's President/CEO/General Manager; ultimate
accountability for SMS effectiveness cannot be delegated even though specific responsibilities
can be. Chief Safety Officer (CSO) or SMS Executive: may be designated by the Accountable
Executive for day-to-day SMS implementation, reporting directly to the Accountable Executive
(who may also serve in this role themselves).

ORGANIZATIONAL ACCIDENTS -- CASE STUDIES CITED
The report cites the 2005 BP Texas City Refinery disaster (15 killed, 180 injured, over $1.5
billion in losses) as an example where the US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board
found BP "lacked focus on controlling major hazard risks," rewarding personal safety metrics
over process safety, with ineffective leadership oversight. It also cites the MTA's August 2014
Blue-Ribbon Panel (BRP) report, convened after mainline derailments at LIRR, Metro-North
Railroad, and NYC Transit involving track-related defects, which recommended restructuring so
each rail agency's Chief Safety Officer reports directly to agency leadership with no other job
responsibilities besides safety.

SAFETY CULTURE -- FIVE COMPONENTS (adapted from James Reason)
An informed culture (current knowledge of human/technical/organizational/environmental safety
factors); a reporting culture (encourages error and near-miss reporting via confidential systems
with fast feedback); a learning culture (willingness/competence to learn from safety data); a
flexible culture (can reconfigure and adapt, even from hierarchical to flatter structures); and a just
culture (avoids individual blame, focuses on systemic deficiencies, distinguishes error from
violation).

EMPLOYEE SAFETY REPORTING EXAMPLES
FRA's Confidential Close Call Reporting System (C3RS): a NASA-FRA partnership (NASA
serves as an independent "honest broker" with no regulatory interest, drawing on its experience
running the Aviation Safety Reporting System since 1976 with over one million reports). As of
the report's writing, C3RS had 9 participating carriers, 18 active Peer Review Teams (PRTs), and
over 21,000 eligible frontline railroad employees. A 12-year FRA evaluation across four
demonstration sites found C3RS reduced derailments (3 sites), injuries (1 site), discipline hearings
(2 sites), and improved safety culture (4 sites). A separate close-call program operated by the
Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) with WMATA lets employees report voluntarily under
Confidential Information Protection and Statistical Efficiency Act (CIPSEA) protections.

A NYCT track safety taskforce survey found more than half of maintenance-of-way personnel had
experienced a near miss or close call, but only about one-third of those who had experienced one
reported it -- attributed to a "blame culture."

JOINT UNION-MANAGEMENT SAFETY EFFORTS
After two roadway worker fatal accidents in one week in April 2007, NYCT convened a Joint
Track Safety Taskforce including management and Transport Workers Union (TWU) Local 100
representatives; it made 63 recommendations, and subsequent joint labor-management audits
correlated with declining rules non-compliance. NTSB's Special Investigation Report on Roadway
Worker Protection (NTSB/SIR 14-03, 2014) noted that "union representation brings operations-
specific knowledge to the accident investigation team and helps facilitate the cooperation of
employees."

HAZARD IDENTIFICATION METHODOLOGY
A hazard is "any real or potential condition that can cause injury, illness, or death; damage to or
loss of the facilities, equipment, rolling stock, or infrastructure of a public transportation system;
or damage to the environment" (49 CFR 673.5). Three methodologies for identifying hazards:
Reactive (analysis of past events/accidents), Proactive (analysis of existing/real-time situations via
employee reporting or monitoring), and Predictive (data-gathering to anticipate future adverse
events before they occur).

The report uses an "iceberg graph" (based on H.W. Heinrich's 1930s industrial safety research) to
illustrate that for every highly damaging accident at the tip of the iceberg, there is a much larger
number of less-harmful precursor incidents, and an even larger number of "normal" work
situations containing hazards below the surface -- hazard identification is most effective at that
base, before consequences ever occur.

HAZARD ANALYSIS METHODS
Operating Hazard Analysis (OHA): identifies hazards from personnel/procedure interactions
during production, testing, operations, maintenance, and emergencies.
Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA): determines effects of sub-element failures on
system operation and classifies each by severity; strengths include identifying single-point
failure modes needing design redundancy, but limited to evaluating single failure modes at a time
and can become unwieldy for complex multi-layered systems.
Fault Tree Analysis (FTA method, distinct from the Federal Transit Administration): a
deductive method working backward from an undesired top event to find plausible causes;
strong for systems with many interfaces, but is a static model that cannot address time
interdependencies or partial/conditional failure states.
Software Safety Analysis: addresses hazards from software controlling bus engine systems,
door failures, CCTV, and other computer-interfaced systems, per Military Standard MIL-STD-882E.

"PRACTICAL DRIFT" CONCEPT
Practical drift is "the slow and inconspicuous, yet steady, uncoupling between written procedures
and actual practices" during service delivery (drawing on Scott Snook's "Friendly Fire" analysis
of the accidental 1994 Blackhawk shootdown). It is not always negative (adaptations can be more
efficient or safer), but unmonitored drift can create unrecognized safety risk. Example given: an
operator skips steps of a pre-trip inspection due to bad weather or fatigue; if unnoticed and
uncorrected, other operators may adopt the same shortcut until inspections degrade into a
"pencil-whipping exercise."

SAFETY RISK ASSESSMENT PROCESS (five steps)
(1) Evaluate probability of the hazard's consequence occurring; (2) evaluate severity of the
consequence if it occurs; (3) evaluate current mitigations already in place; (4) index the safety
risk by combining probability and severity into a Risk Assessment Code (RAC); (5) determine
acceptability of the resulting risk level.

Event probability levels range from A (Frequent, likely to occur often) through E (Improbable,
assumed the event may never be experienced) to F (Eliminated, hazard identified and later
removed). Event severity categories range from Catastrophic (1: death, permanent total
disability, or monetary loss >=$10M) through Critical (2: >=$1M but <$10M, hospitalization of
3+ personnel) and Marginal (3: >=$100K but <$1M, lost workday injury) to Negligible (4: <$100K,
no lost workday).

MITIGATION HIERARCHY (most to least effective)
Design for Minimum Hazard (eliminate at design phase) > Safety Devices (reduce consequence
severity when the hazard can't be eliminated) > Warning Devices (detect and alert when
elimination isn't possible) > Special Procedures (administrative controls when no better option
exists). The report cautions that administrative action (memos, updated SOPs, discipline, retraining)
is the cheapest and most common "quick fix" response to incidents, but over-reliance on it can
create the appearance that something has been done without resolving the underlying hazard.
