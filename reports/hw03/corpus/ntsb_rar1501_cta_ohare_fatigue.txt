NTSB/RAR-15-01
Chicago Transit Authority Train Collides with Bumping Post and Escalator at O'Hare Station
Chicago, Illinois, March 24, 2014. Adopted: April 28, 2015.
Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/accidentreports/reports/rar1501.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-19 (verified live fetch)

ABSTRACT
About 2:49 a.m. on March 24, 2014, Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) train No. 141 collided
with the bumping post near the end of the center pocket track at O'Hare Station. The lead car
rode over the bumping post and went up an escalator at the end of the track that provided public
access to O'Hare International Airport; no one was using the escalator at the time. About 50
people were on the train. Thirty-three injured passengers and the injured train operator were
taken to the hospital. Estimated damage was $11,196,796.

ACCIDENT NARRATIVE
The train operator went on duty at 8:40 p.m. on March 23, 2014, for her fourth of five scheduled
round trips between O'Hare and Logan Square stations. She told investigators she was not sleepy
at the start of her shift, but at Harlem Station (about 10 minutes from O'Hare) she turned off the
cab heater because she felt warm and drowsy. As the train entered O'Hare Station on the center
pocket track at about 25 mph, she fell asleep, telling investigators: "I was tired. I didn't get any
sleep the day before... I nodded off into the terminal at O'Hare." The train passed a red wayside
signal without braking; a fixed track trip 29 feet 2 inches from the bumping post mechanically
triggered full dynamic and friction braking, which woke the operator, but there was insufficient
distance remaining -- the train struck the bumping post and end platform at about 23 mph and rode
up the escalator.

STATION DESIGN DEFICIENCY
The center pocket track's bumping post was installed with only about 15.6 feet of clearance to
the end of track (versus about 60 feet for the two adjacent tracks), with no room for the "energy
friction shoes" installed on the other tracks to help slow a striking train. Original 1980 design
specifications called for the pocket track to be capable of stopping an eight-car train at 15 mph,
but the track's cab speed was actually set at 25 mph -- inconsistent with the design criteria and
with the 15 mph speed used on the other two tracks. Following the accident, CTA reduced the
cab speed to 0 mph in the pocket track and identified similar terminal-track configurations at
Midway, Linden, and Cottage Grove stations for correction.

OPERATOR FATIGUE ANALYSIS
The 25-year-old operator was on the "extra board" (called in as needed) and worked her 12th
consecutive calendar day, including six night-shift starts (7:30-9:00 p.m.) in the week before the
accident. Her 72-hour sleep/wake history showed she likely slept no more than about 10 hours in
the 63 hours preceding the accident. She had received a written warning on February 1, 2014,
for previously dozing off and missing a station stop. The accident occurred at 2:49 a.m., within
the "circadian low" window (roughly 3-5 a.m.) when human alertness is naturally lowest.
Toxicology was negative for alcohol and drugs; her personal electronic device showed no use
during the accident trip. CTA discharged her following the accident.

CTA SCHEDULING POLICY AT THE TIME
CTA imposed no limit on the number of consecutive days or double shifts an employee could
work; the union contract (Amalgamated Transit Union Local 308) only capped any single
assignment at 13 hours. CTA administrative managers were not required to take fatigue
awareness training. Only 24 percent of transit agencies nationally, per a 2006 FTA survey,
addressed fatigue management in their System Safety Program Plans. Following the accident,
CTA: capped actual train-operation duty at 12 hours within a 14-hour window; increased minimum
rest between shifts from 8 to 10 hours; required at least 1 day off in any 7-day period; and limited
new operators (under 1 year experience) to 32 hours of actual train-operating duty per week.

SIGNAL SYSTEM AND TRANSMISSION-BASED TRAIN CONTROL (TBTC)
The report concludes a fully implemented Transmission-Based Train Control (TBTC) system
-- the transit-industry equivalent of freight rail's Positive Train Control (PTC) -- would have
prevented the accident by enforcing braking before the train could pass the red signal, regardless
of operator action. The NTSB noted that Safety Recommendation R-09-08 (issued in 2009 after a
similar 2008 MBTA Green Line rear-end collision in Newton, MA) asking FTA to facilitate PTC
implementation on transit systems had produced no substantive action in the five years since,
and was reclassified "Closed-Unacceptable Action/Superseded" in this report, replaced by new
Recommendation R-15-22 requiring TBTC systems that prevent train collisions.

EVENT RECORDER HISTORY
The 2600-series cars involved (rebuilt 1999-2002, originally manufactured 1981-1987) were not
equipped with true event recorders, only a "fault logger" that was destroyed in the crash, so no
event data could be recovered. The NTSB traced a 19-year history of unsuccessful attempts to
require event recorders on transit rail vehicles: a 1996 recommendation (R-96-46) was closed as
"Acceptable Action" after FTA issued guidance but did not mandate installation; a 2002
recommendation (R-02-19) following two 2001 CTA rear-end collisions was closed
"Unacceptable Action" in 2008 because FTA said it lacked legislative authority to regulate
vehicle equipment. Only after the 2012 Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act
(MAP-21) granted FTA new equipment-standard authority did this report renew the
recommendation (R-15-23), this time citing FTA's now-clear legal authority to act.

OVERSIGHT CONTEXT (FTA/MAP-21)
This report traces the same FTA statutory-authority gap discussed in the 2015 L'Enfant Plaza
oversight letter and Fort Totten report elsewhere in this corpus: it cites the NTSB's 2009 Fort
Totten investigation and the FTA's own 2010 acknowledgment that "rail transit operates in a
significantly less regulated environment with regard to safety" compared to airlines or Amtrak,
despite carrying more daily passengers. MAP-21 (effective October 1, 2012) finally gave FTA
authority under 49 U.S.C. Section 5329 to establish a National Public Transportation Safety Plan
and adopt a Safety Management System (SMS) approach nationally.

PROBABLE CAUSE
The National Transportation Safety Board determines that the probable cause of the accident
was the failure of the train operator to stop the train at the appropriate signal due to falling asleep
as a result of fatigue, resulting from the challenges of working shiftwork, circadian factors, and
acute sleep loss from her ineffective off-duty time management. Chicago Transit Authority
failed to effectively manage the operator's work schedule to mitigate fatigue risk. Contributing
to the severity of the accident was CTA's failure to identify the insufficient stopping distance and
inadequate speed restriction at the center pocket track at O'Hare Station.

ADDITIONAL VERIFIED DETAIL: SAFETY CERTIFICATION AND CONFIGURATION MANAGEMENT
The O'Hare Station itself was designed and built in the 1980s by the City of Chicago, and no
documentation existed showing a safety certification of the original station design -- something
that would be required today under 49 CFR 659 for a project of this size. At the time of the 2014
accident, CTA's own internal policy only required its Configuration Management (safety-review)
process to be triggered for capital projects costing $1 million or more, and full Safety
Certification only for projects costing $100 million or more. Following the accident, CTA's chief
engineer for infrastructure told investigators the agency had hired a consultant specifically to
restructure its contract requirements so that safety certification review would apply even to
smaller projects, and that all future designs -- regardless of size -- would be routed through CTA's
Safety Department for review, including input from the Operations Department. This gap
(safety-critical design changes below a high dollar threshold escaping formal safety review)
mirrors, in a different agency and mode, the pattern the NTSB found in the WMATA Smithsonian-
area derailment discussed in this corpus's WMATA history file, where a known "wide gage"
track defect was left in service without triggering any of WMATA's own safety escalation
procedures.

ADDITIONAL VERIFIED DETAIL: TRACK STOP TERMINOLOGY
CTA's fixed-block signal system, as this report describes it, uses "track trips" -- physical
mechanisms in the raised or "tripping" position at a red signal that mechanically engage a lever
on the underside of a passing train to trigger emergency braking if the train passes a stop signal
without authorization. This is functionally similar to, but a different specific mechanism from,
the electronic "program stop" and "overspeed/overshoot flag" system used by the driverless
Miami International Airport APM system described elsewhere in this corpus -- illustrating that
different rail transit technologies (conventional heavy rail with human operators, versus a fully
automated guideway system) use entirely different physical and electronic means to achieve the
same basic safety goal of preventing a train from overrunning its intended stopping point.
