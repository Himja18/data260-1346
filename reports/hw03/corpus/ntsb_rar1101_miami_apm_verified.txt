NTSB/RAR-11/01
Miami International Airport, Automated People Mover Train Collision with Passenger
Terminal Wall, Miami, Florida, November 28, 2008. Adopted: November 8, 2011.
Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/RAR1101.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-19 (verified live fetch)

ABSTRACT
About 4:44 p.m. on November 28, 2008, a three-car automated people mover (APM) train
operating along a fixed guideway on E Concourse at Miami International Airport failed to stop
at the passenger platform and struck a wall at the end of the guideway. A maintenance technician
was monitoring the train from the lead car, but it was operating in fully automatic mode without
a human operator. The technician and five passengers were injured; one person on the platform
also required medical attention.

THE JUMPER WIRE
The south train had been experiencing recurring failures with its wayside antenna, dynamic
braking, and overspeed/overshoot systems for weeks before the accident. On November 21,
2008 (one week before the accident), a JCI (Johnson Controls, Inc.) maintenance technician
installed a "jumper wire" between two terminals in the train's control compartment to keep it in
service while troubleshooting continued. This jumper wire bypassed the vital (safety-critical)
overspeed/overshoot relay -- the fail-safe backup system designed to stop the train if the primary
"program stop" positioning system ever failed. One JCI technician who reviewed the schematics
realized the jumper wire disabled this safety backup and told his shift lead, who directed him to
leave it in place anyway so the train could keep running. JCI had no written procedures governing
when jumper wires could be used or requiring their removal before passenger service.

WHAT ACTUALLY FAILED
Postaccident testing found the failure was a broken crystal inside the program stop module's
oscillator circuit -- the component that counted phase changes in a wayside antenna to determine
the train's position relative to its stopping point. Without a working oscillator, the train had no
way to trigger deceleration. Normally the overspeed/overshoot backup system would have caught
this failure and applied emergency brakes, but the jumper wire had disabled exactly that backup.

MAINTENANCE HISTORY AND TESTING GAPS
JCI took over APM maintenance from the original contractor (Bombardier, and predecessors
back to the original 1980 manufacturer Westinghouse) in January 2008, after Bombardier
declined a new maintenance contract over liability concerns about the trains being past their
"design life." No stopping/braking tests had been conducted on either APM train before the
accident because JCI was concerned about placing stress on the aging undercarriages. The
manufacturer recommended testing vital relays every 4 years; the overspeed/overshoot relay that
had been bypassed was tagged as last inspected on December 4, 2001 -- nearly 7 years before the
accident.

POSTACCIDENT VERIFICATION TESTING
NTSB investigators conducted five stopping tests on the (undamaged) north train on December
7, 2008, WITHOUT a jumper wire installed, and the train performed within design parameters in
every test -- including correctly triggering emergency stops when simulated door-opening and
overspeed conditions were introduced. This confirmed the overspeed/overshoot system itself was
functional and would have prevented the accident had it not been bypassed.

SAFETY OVERSIGHT GAP -- A THIRD LAYER OF FAILURE
Beyond the FTA/State Safety Oversight Agency (SSOA) framework discussed elsewhere in this
corpus for FTA-FUNDED rail transit, this report reveals a distinct gap: the Miami APM system
received NO FTA funding and was therefore not covered by 49 CFR Part 659 at all. Florida's
own oversight statute (Section 341.061) was not enacted until 1984 and was not retroactive, so in
a 1988 exchange of letters, the Miami-Dade Aviation Department (MDAD) successfully argued
it did not need to develop a system safety program plan for this specific APM (built in 1980), and
no further correspondence on the topic occurred between 1988 and the 2008 accident -- 20 years
with essentially no external safety oversight of this system. The report's Appendix B lists at least
22 other states with fixed-guideway systems (mostly airport people movers and amusement-park
monorails/funiculars) similarly falling outside any state oversight agency's regulated scope.

ORGANIZATIONAL ACCIDENT FRAMEWORK
The report explicitly applies James Reason's "organizational accident" framework (also cited in
the FTA bus accident investigation background research document elsewhere in this corpus),
distinguishing "active failures" (the jumper wire installation itself) from "latent failures" (MDAD's
and Florida's absent oversight, which had been dormant for two decades before this specific
active failure triggered the accident).

PROBABLE CAUSE
The National Transportation Safety Board determines that the probable cause of this accident
was the installation by Johnson Controls, Inc. maintenance technicians of a jumper wire that
prevented the overspeed/overshoot system from activating to stop the train when the crystal
within the primary program stop module failed. Contributing factors: (1) JCI's failure to provide
specific procedures regarding disabling vital train control systems during passenger operations,
(2) ineffective safety oversight by the Miami-Dade Aviation Department, (3) lack of adequate
safety oversight by the state of Florida, and (4) lack of authority by the U.S. DOT to provide
adequate safety oversight of such systems.

RECOMMENDATIONS
New recommendations went to the U.S. DOT and all 50 states/D.C. (jointly identify all fixed
guideway systems within each jurisdiction, R-11-1/R-11-2), to all 50 states/D.C. (obtain statutory
authority to oversee ALL fixed guideway systems regardless of funding source or age, R-11-3),
to Miami-Dade County (develop a system safety program plan, R-11-4), and to Johnson Controls,
Inc. (prohibit deactivation of safety-critical systems during passenger service, R-11-5). The
report also reiterates Recommendation R-10-3 (from the Fort Totten investigation, also in this
corpus) urging the DOT to seek broader rail transit safety oversight authority.
