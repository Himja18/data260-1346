NTSB Safety Recommendation Status Classifications — Cross-Reference Notes
Compiled from status labels used across multiple verified NTSB reports in this corpus
(Fort Totten, O'Hare/CTA, Miami APM, L'Enfant Plaza oversight letter).
Accessed: 2026-09-19

WHAT THE STATUS LABELS MEAN
Every NTSB safety recommendation carries a status the agency updates as the addressee
responds. "Open — Acceptable Response" means the addressee agreed and has a credible plan
underway, even if work isn't finished. "Open — Unacceptable Response" means the addressee's
reply so far does not adequately address the safety issue. "Closed — Acceptable Action" means
the NTSB is satisfied the addressee's completed action resolves the safety issue. "Closed —
Unacceptable Action/Superseded" means the NTSB gave up on the original recommendation
working as intended and issued a new, revised recommendation in its place — this is the
designation used, in this corpus, when NTSB replaced 2009's Recommendation R-09-08 (asking
FTA to "facilitate" positive train control) with 2015's more specific R-15-22 (requiring transit
agencies to actually implement train-control systems that prevent collisions), after five years of
inconclusive FTA responses following the 2008 MBTA Newton, MA collision.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR READING THIS CORPUS
A recommendation's status is time-stamped to whenever the citing report was written — so two
reports in this corpus can describe the same recommendation's status differently, without either
one being wrong, simply because they were adopted years apart. Readers cross-referencing this
corpus's WMATA-related documents (Shady Grove, Fort Totten, and the L'Enfant Plaza letter)
should treat a status label as accurate only as of that specific document's adoption date, not as a
permanently fixed fact about the recommendation.

RECURRING PATTERN ACROSS THIS CORPUS'S VERIFIED REPORTS
Multiple recommendations in this corpus's source documents follow a similar arc: an initial
recommendation after one accident goes "Open" for years with limited follow-through, then a
second, unrelated accident elsewhere prompts the NTSB to revisit the same underlying safety
gap and either reissue or sharpen the original recommendation. This pattern appears twice in this
corpus's verified primary sources: once in the event-recorder recommendation history described
in the O'Hare (CTA) report (a 1996 recommendation partially addressed by 2008, only fully
renewed after MAP-21 gave FTA clearer authority in 2012), and once in the positive train
control recommendation history spanning the 2008 Newton, MA collision through the 2014
O'Hare collision.

ADDITIONAL NOTE ON THE MAP-21 TRANSITION
The Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act (MAP-21), effective October 1, 2012,
is the single legislative turning point referenced across nearly every recommendation-status
discussion in this corpus's verified sources. Before MAP-21, the Federal Transit Administration
lacked explicit statutory authority to mandate vehicle equipment standards or safety practices on
rail transit systems — it could only encourage, fund research, or convene stakeholders, which is
why so many pre-2012 recommendations in this corpus's reports sit in "Open" status for years at
a time regardless of how serious the underlying safety issue was. After MAP-21, FTA gained
authority under 49 U.S.C. Section 5329 to require a national Public Transportation Safety Plan
and adopt Safety Management System requirements agency-wide, which is the direct cause of
several recommendations in this corpus being reissued in sharper, more specific form in 2015
rather than simply staying open indefinitely.
