Municipal Transit Incidents - Domain Terminology FAQ
A reference document synthesizing recurring questions about terminology differences across
the source documents in this corpus (Domain 2: Municipal Transit Incidents).
Source: Synthesized from cross-referencing all primary source documents in this corpus (see
individual files for original URLs and access dates).
Accessed: 2026-09-19

Q: What is the difference between a "collision," a "derailment," and a "personal casualty" in
NTD reporting terminology?
A: Per the FTA's Safety & Security Policy Manual (2026) and the older SAMIS glossary (1997),
a collision involves impact between a transit vehicle and something else (another vehicle, a
person, an object, an animal, a rail vehicle, a vessel, or a dock). A derailment is explicitly a
non-collision event -- a train or rail vehicle unintentionally leaves its rails (this also covers a bus
"going off road" in the older SAMIS bus-mode terminology, and rollovers). A personal casualty
is a distinct category for people hurt on transit property or vehicles NOT as the result of a
collision, derailment, or fire -- for example, someone falling on an escalator, tripping while
boarding, or being injured in a station parking lot.

Q: Why do different reports in this corpus give different numbers for "how many WMATA
accidents the NTSB has investigated"?
A: Because the count depends on when the statement was made and what is being counted. The
1996 Shady Grove report references prior WMATA investigations without a specific running
total. The 2015 L'Enfant Plaza oversight letter states 11 NTSB investigations of the WMATA rail
system specifically over the prior 33 years (i.e., since roughly 1982), with 18 total fatalities
across those investigations -- a count that does NOT include earlier special studies (like the 1970
pre-construction study) or bus-mode incidents, since those aren't "rail system" investigations in
the narrow sense used in that letter.

Q: Is "Municipal Transit Incidents" limited to trains, or does it include buses?
A: Based on the documents in this corpus, the domain spans both. The FTA's NTD reporting
system and its SAMIS/NTST statistical publications track Motor Bus (MB) alongside all rail
modes as co-equal categories under the same Safety & Security reporting framework. Several
individual incident reports in this corpus involve buses specifically rather than rail (the NYC
MTA transit bus pedestrian fatality, HAB-18/10; the two school bus/grade-crossing collision
reports, Fox River Grove and Conasauga, though those involve school buses rather than public
transit buses specifically). The FTA-sponsored "Transit Bus Accident Investigations" background
research document treats bus-mode safety management as a full peer topic to rail-mode safety
management, using the same Safety Management System (SMS) framework required under 49
CFR Part 673 for all modes.

Q: What counts as a "reportable" event versus something transit agencies track internally but
don't have to report to the federal government?
A: This threshold has shifted over time and varies by mode, as detailed in fta_ntd_safety_
security_policy_manual_2026.txt and the cross-document reconciliation note in
fta_ntd_glossary_and_definitions_supplement.txt. As of the 2026 manual, non-rail modes report
property damage of $25,000 or more; rail modes instead use a "substantial damage" test tied to
operational disruption rather than a dollar figure. The 1997 SAMIS glossary used a flat $1,000
property-damage threshold for ALL modes -- meaning a comparison of "how many incidents
happened in 1997 vs. 2026" cannot be made by simply comparing raw incident counts, because
the definition of what qualifies as a reportable incident has moved substantially over that period.

Q: What does "R-10-3" refer to, and why does it show up in multiple unrelated reports?
A: R-10-3 is an NTSB safety recommendation number issued to the U.S. Department of
Transportation following the 2009 WMATA Fort Totten collision, urging DOT to continue
seeking authority to provide safety oversight of rail fixed guideway transportation systems,
including the ability to promulgate and enforce safety regulations. Because this recommendation
addressed a general statutory-authority gap rather than a WMATA-specific defect, the NTSB
reiterated the same recommendation number in its 2011 Miami International Airport automated
people mover report, even though that accident involved a completely different transit agency,
state, and even transit mode (an automated guideway system rather than a conventional
heavy-rail system). Readers of this corpus should not assume a recommendation number always
maps to a single accident report; some recommendation numbers, like R-10-3, are deliberately
carried forward and cited across multiple, otherwise unrelated investigations until the underlying
systemic issue is considered resolved.

Q: How does "Automated Guideway" (AG) mode in NTD reporting relate to airport people
movers like the Miami APM system in this corpus?
A: The FTA's National Transit Database mode taxonomy includes Automated Guideway (AG) as
a distinct mode covering driverless, fixed-guideway systems -- this is the mode category that
would technically apply to a system like the Miami International Airport people mover if it were
an FTA reporter. However, as the Miami APM accident report documents, that specific system
received no FTA funding and therefore was never an NTD reporter at all, illustrating that not
every fixed-guideway transit-like system in the United States falls within the NTD's data
collection scope, even though it may be functionally identical to systems that do.

Q: Why do some reports in this corpus describe circadian/fatigue factors (CTA O'Hare) while
others describe purely mechanical or procedural failures (Miami APM, Fort Totten)?
A: These represent different points along the "organizational accident" causal chain described in
the FTA bus accident investigations background research document in this corpus: some
accidents in this corpus trace primarily to human-factors failures at the front line (the O'Hare
operator's fatigue), some to procedural/maintenance failures (the Miami APM jumper wire,
installed against at least one technician's own better judgment), and some to failures within the
technical equipment itself compounded by organizational failure to catch it (the Fort Totten track
circuit's parasitic oscillation, present for over two decades before causing an accident). A
retrieval system asked broadly "what causes transit accidents in this corpus" should ideally
surface examples from more than one of these causal categories rather than over-indexing on
whichever category has the most documents.
