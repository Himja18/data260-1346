OVERVIEW
The 2021 NTST discusses data from Report Years (RY) 2012 to 2021, reflecting the impact of the
COVID-19 public health emergency (declared March 13, 2020) on national transit data. April
2020 is the lowest month on record (dating to 2002) for national urban public transportation
ridership, with 158.5 million Unlinked Passenger Trips (UPT) taken, compared to 835.1 million
UPT in April 2019 -- an 81 percent year-over-year decrease. The year-over-year decrease from
December 2019 to December 2020 was 62 percent across all modes for UPT; there was a 48
percent increase across all modes for UPT from December 2020 to December 2021 as recovery
began.

RIDERSHIP TRENDS 2012-2021
Total urban transit ridership decreased 57.5 percent over the 10-year period, from about 10.36
billion passengers (2012) to 4.40 billion passengers (2021). Approximately 87.3 percent of urban
agency ridership occurs on Bus and Heavy Rail services combined. By mode, 2012-2021 ridership
declines were: Bus -57.1%, Heavy Rail -57.1%, Commuter Rail -68.4%, Light Rail -60.6%, Other
-55.0%, Demand Response -38.3% (smallest decline of any mode), Vanpool -55.5%.

Rail ridership decreased from 4.75 billion to 1.96 billion UPT over the decade; rail's share of
national ridership decreased from 45.3 percent (2012) to 43.8 percent (2021). Within rail, Heavy
Rail makes up about 82.1 percent of total rail ridership in 2021 (up slightly from 78.5 percent
average 2011-2019), Light Rail about 9 percent, and Commuter Rail about 7.6 percent.

EMPLOYMENT AND FUNDING
At the end of Calendar Year 2021, there were over 370,000 employees (339,067 urban, 29,750
rural, 1,269 tribal) involved in operating, administering, and maintaining U.S. public transit.

Federal funding was the predominant operating expense funding source in 2021 due to COVID-19
relief programs: directly generated revenues (fares, etc.) funded only 17.4 percent of urban
operating expenses; Local funded 25.6 percent; State funded 20.8 percent; Federal government
sources funded the remaining 36.2 percent. In RY 2021, 852 transit agencies spent over $13.1
billion from CARES, CRRSA, and ARP pandemic relief programs combined, mostly ($4.957
billion, 73.0% of total federal COVID relief) on operating expenses -- a 95 percent increase over
RY 2020 relief spending.

Bus modes used the largest share of total industry operating expenses at $23.1 billion, followed by
Heavy Rail ($9.4 billion) and Commuter Rail ($6.5 billion). Vehicle Operations accounted for
48.41 percent of all operating expenses (functional breakdown: Vehicle Operations $22,944M,
Vehicle Maintenance $8,954M, Facility Maintenance $6,108M, General Administration $9,390M;
total $47,396M).

FAREBOX RECOVERY
In 2021, for each dollar spent on operating costs per trip across all modes, only 12.8 cents were
recovered through fares -- a 30 percent decrease from the 2020 fare recovery ratio of 18.4 cents,
driven by the pandemic. Commuter Rail, Commuter Bus, and Vanpool have relatively high
farebox recovery ratios (scheduled around commuter demand, limited off-peak service); Vanpool
in particular has a high ratio because drivers are unpaid volunteers and the mode has traditionally
relied on rider fees with limited government subsidy.

COST AND SERVICE CHARACTERISTICS (2021)
Average trip length: Vanpool 39.0 miles, Commuter Rail 25.0 miles, Commuter Bus 24.6 miles
(all long-distance commuter-focused modes) vs. much shorter trips on fixed-route bus and other
rail modes; Heavy Rail average trip length was 4.6 miles.
Cost per UPT: Commuter Rail $43.90 (one of the highest per-trip rates) but cost per Passenger
Mile Traveled (PMT) only $1.75 (one of the lowest), because trips are so long; Bus had a low cost
per UPT ($10.36) but a relatively higher cost per PMT ($2.84) because trips are short (3.7 mile
average).
Passengers per hour (PPH): Ferryboat highest at 93.5 PPH, Heavy Rail 49.6 PPH, Demand
Response lowest at 1.6 PPH.
Cost per vehicle revenue hour: Ferryboat highest at $1,925.68/hour; Vanpool lowest at
$39.49/hour (again reflecting unpaid volunteer drivers).
Average fleet age: Vanpool 3.56 years (typical useful life 4-5 years); Bus 7.48 years (typical useful
life 10-12 years); rail vehicles designed for 25-35 year service life, explaining their higher average
ages.

FIXED GUIDEWAY INFRASTRUCTURE
Rail transit modes operate 74.9 percent of total Fixed Guideway (FG) and High-Intensity Busway
(HIB) Directional Route Miles (DRM) nationally. Seven of the most populous UZAs (all in the top
15 in population in every census since 1860) account for over 46.9 percent (8,251.9 miles) of all
FG route miles nationally, averaging 1,179 FG miles per UZA -- versus just 92.5 average FG miles
for the 101 other UZAs that have any FG at all.

SAFETY AND SECURITY DATA (2021, NTD-reported, preliminary)
The total number of transit fatalities has been reasonably stable at about 322 annual fatalities
nationally, with year-to-year variation of less than 50 total fatalities. The fatality rate increased
from about 0.79 fatalities per 10 million Vehicle Revenue Miles (VRM) in 2020 to about 0.94
fatalities per 10 million VRM in 2021. In 2021, rail modes had higher fatality rates than non-rail
modes, in part because suicides are much more common on rail than non-rail modes.

Large Urbanized Areas (UZAs) experienced 246.70 reportable safety and security events per 100
million VRM in 2021 -- notably higher than Medium UZAs (164.64 per 100 million VRM) and
Small UZAs (100.63 per 100 million VRM), suggesting event rates scale with urban density and
system complexity rather than staying constant.

Note on data comparability: the NTD's reporting requirements for "Safety Occurrences Not
Otherwise Classified" events changed in 2015, causing an increase in reported injuries from 2015
onward compared to 2011-2014 that reflects a reporting-definition change rather than an actual
safety trend -- an important caveat when comparing pre- and post-2015 injury statistics.

Note on FRA/NTD scope split: the Federal Railroad Administration (not FTA/NTD) oversees
safety for Commuter Rail systems, the Alaska Railroad, and a select set of Hybrid Rail and Heavy
Rail systems; these agencies do NOT report safety data to the NTD and are excluded from NTD
safety analyses -- meaning NTD-derived safety statistics understate total rail safety events
nationally by excluding these FRA-regulated systems.
