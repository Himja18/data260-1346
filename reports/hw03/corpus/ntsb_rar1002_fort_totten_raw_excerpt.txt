PERSONNEL INFORMATION -- TRAIN 112 OPERATOR (verbatim detail)
WMATA records showed that the operator of the striking train was hired initially as a bus
operator on January 22, 2007. On December 8, 2008, she was reclassified as a train operator, the
position she occupied at the time of the accident. She began her rail training on December 8,
2008, and successfully completed the training program on February 11, 2009. WMATA files
disclosed no disciplinary action as a train operator prior to the accident.

Examination of the operator's cell phone records revealed that between 12:37:37 a.m. and
4:14:58 p.m. on the day of the accident, 11 voice calls were made from her cell phone, and 9
were received. The last activity was an 80-second outgoing call made at 4:14:58 p.m., about 18
minutes before departure from the Glenmont station and 45 minutes before the collision. The
records showed no cell phone activity during the time the operator was responsible for operating
the train on the day of the accident.

PERSONNEL INFORMATION -- TRAIN 214 OPERATOR (verbatim detail)
WMATA records indicated that the operator of the struck train was hired initially as a bus driver
on February 17, 2000. On October 26, 2001, he was reclassified as a train operator. Efficiency
test records showed that the operator of train 214 had received three reprimands and two
suspensions since 2003: two reprimands in February 2003 (a 6-minute delay and failing to report
a station overrun), a third reprimand on May 22, 2009 for failing to take his key before securing a
car, a 2004 suspension for passing a red signal on the main track, and a 2006 suspension for
making an improper coupling that caused equipment damage. This operator was also removed
from service on three occasions in August 2008 for manually stopping his 6-car train at the 8-car
marker at passenger stations rather than relying on the automated system.

On the day of the accident, WMATA required all revenue trains to be operated in automatic mode
during the morning and evening rush periods per Metrorail Safety Rules and Procedures
Handbook (MSRPH) Rule 3.20. Contrary to instructions to return to automatic mode, the operator
of train 214 continued to operate his train manually, citing MSRPH Rule 3.1: "Passenger safety
is the responsibility of every WMATA employee; however, the Train Operators have the ultimate
and final responsibility for the safety of the passengers on their particular trains."

MEDICAL AND TOXICOLOGICAL INFORMATION (verbatim detail)
Postaccident toxicological testing was conducted between 8:17 p.m. and 9:10 p.m. on the day of
the accident for three OCC employees on duty at the time (an assistant superintendent, an OCC
supervisor, and a Red Line controller), testing for ethyl alcohol and illegal drugs (marijuana
metabolites, cocaine metabolites, opiates, amphetamines, phencyclidine). All results were
negative. Specimens from the operator of train 214 were sent to the Civil Aeromedical Institute
(CAMI) for independent analysis, testing for ethanol, amphetamines, opiates, marijuana, cocaine,
phencyclidine, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, antidepressants, antihistamines, meprobamate,
methaqualone, and nicotine -- all negative. The NTSB, using its own authority (since the FTA has
no mechanism for obtaining specimens from an employee killed in an accident), obtained
specimens from the autopsy of the operator of train 112; results were negative for alcohol and
other drugs, though ibuprofen was detected in the blood specimen.

METEOROLOGICAL INFORMATION
The accident occurred during daylight. The weather was reported as dry with clear skies and
10-mile visibility. The wind was at 13 mph, and the temperature was 84 degrees F.

AUTOMATIC TRAIN CONTROL SYSTEM -- DETAILED DESIGN
The Metrorail ATC system consists of three subsystems: Automatic Train Protection (ATP),
which is a wayside system providing collision and overspeed protection through automatic block
signaling and transmits speed commands based on instantaneous track occupancy; Automatic
Train Supervision (ATS), primarily a wayside system with OCC inputs controlling train routing
and scheduling; and Automatic Train Operation (ATO), primarily a wayside system managing
train startup, acceleration, running speed, and smooth station stops.

Speed commands are displayed on train operators' consoles via three readouts: ATP speed
("limiting" speed, the maximum the ATP subsystem allows), ATS speed ("regulated" speed,
maximum authorized by the rail operations computer), and "train" speed (actual speed). By
design, if a train does not receive speed commands for any reason, the ATP and ATS speeds
default to 0 mph as a fail-safe measure.

Three train operating modes exist: Mode 1 (automatic) -- normal functions controlled by ATC,
operator monitors; the striking train (112) was in this mode. Mode 2, Level 1 (manual with speed
protection) -- operator controls acceleration/braking with ATP overspeed protection; the struck
train (214) was in this mode. Mode 3 (manual with ATP cutout) -- no overspeed protection, used
only when malfunctioning equipment makes Modes 1 or 2 impossible.

TRACK CIRCUIT DETECTION MECHANISM (verbatim technical detail)
At each end of each track circuit, an impedance bond is mounted between the rails; one end acts
as transmitter, the other as receiver. A track circuit transmitter module generates a code-rate
modulated audio frequency signal supplied to the transmitter impedance bond, which injects it
into the rails. If no train occupies the block, the signal travels through the rails to the receiver
impedance bond, which sends it back to a track circuit receiver module. If the signal meets
amplitude and modulation requirements, the module energizes a track relay coil, causing the
relay armature to "pick up" (rise), indicating the block is unoccupied. When a train enters the
track circuit, its wheelsets shunt the signal away from the receiver bond; when the returned signal
drops below a preset level, the module deenergizes the track relay, signifying occupancy. A loss
of train detection occurs when the relay does not deenergize while a train is occupying the track
circuit -- exactly what happened at track circuit B2-304.

POSTACCIDENT INSPECTION AND TESTING OF ATP COMPONENTS
Control of 14 of the 27 track circuits along track B2 between Takoma and Fort Totten is provided
by track circuit modules mounted on equipment racks in the Fort Totten train control room. The
ATP transmitter/receiver modules installed at Fort Totten at the time of the accident were
manufactured by GRS in the early 1970s. Investigators tested the five track circuits preceding
track circuit B2-304 using a 0.06-ohm shunt to simulate a train wheelset at three locations
(transmitter end, middle, receiver end) of each; all track relays deenergized correctly. Track
circuit B2-304 itself detected a shunt at the transmitter end but failed to detect a shunt placed in
the middle of the circuit, and detection at the receiver end was intermittent -- the track relay
momentarily energized and picked up, then deenergized and dropped out.

DATA MANAGEMENT AND ALARM RATES (verbatim statistic)
According to OCC records, "track-circuit-failed-occupied" alarms (a track circuit reporting
occupied when actually vacant) occur at a rate of about 5,000 per week systemwide, concentrated
in about 100 track circuits (of about 3,000 systemwide). "Track-circuit-failed-vacant" (NRB)
alarms occur at about 3,000 per week. The AIM system creates and then deletes more than 1,000
"ghost trains" each week as part of its nuisance-alarm suppression logic. Because of this high
background rate of routine, non-hazardous track circuit noise, WMATA had classified these
alarm types as "minor" -- a classification the investigation found contributed to the loss-of-
detection anomaly at B2-304 not being caught before the accident.
