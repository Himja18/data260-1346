NTSB Safety Recommendation Letter R-15-31 and R-15-32 (Urgent)
Date: September 30, 2015. To: The Honorable Anthony Foxx, Secretary of Transportation, US DOT.
Subject: WMATA Metrorail smoke and arcing accident, L'Enfant Plaza station, January 12, 2015,
and inadequate safety oversight by the Tri-State Oversight Committee (TOC).
Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-recs/recletters/R-15-031-032.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-19

BACKGROUND -- THE L'ENFANT PLAZA ACCIDENT
On Monday, January 12, 2015, about 3:15 p.m. eastern standard time, WMATA Metrorail Yellow
Line train 302 stopped after encountering an accumulation of heavy smoke while traveling
southbound in a tunnel between the L'Enfant Plaza station and the Potomac River bridge in the
District of Columbia. About 400 passengers were on board the six-car train. The smoke
originated from an arcing event near the third rail about 2,000 feet south of the L'Enfant Plaza
station. Smoke filled the L'Enfant Plaza station, causing an evacuation. District of Columbia Fire
and Emergency Management Services reported that 86 people were treated and transported from
the scene; another 9 passengers self-transported to medical facilities. There was one passenger
fatality (Carol Glover, 61, died of acute respiratory failure).

Passengers spent more than 30 minutes inside the smoke-filled train before firefighters arrived. A
smoke detector near the location of heavy smoke activated at 3:04 p.m. but was not displayed at
the Rail Operations Control Center (ROCC) because of a loose wire preventing communication
with the Advanced Information Management System. WMATA's standard operating procedure
stated that at the first report of smoke, all trains should be stopped in both directions, but this did
not happen on the day of the accident.

This accident was the 13th WMATA rail accident investigated by the NTSB since Metrorail began
operation in 1976, and the NTSB has issued 101 safety recommendations to WMATA since 1970
(dating back to a special study of the system while it was still under construction).

TRI-STATE OVERSIGHT COMMITTEE (TOC) -- STRUCTURAL PROBLEMS
The TOC was established in 1997 by a memorandum of understanding (MOU) between the
Virginia Department of Rail and Public Transportation, the Maryland Department of
Transportation, and the District of Columbia Department of Transportation, composed of six
representatives (two from each jurisdiction). As the designated State Safety Oversight Agency
(SSOA) for WMATA under 49 CFR Part 659, the TOC develops a System Safety Program
Standard but has NO regulatory or enforcement authority (cannot levy civil penalties) and does
not conduct independent inspections of equipment, infrastructure, or operations -- it relies entirely
on WMATA to respond appropriately to safety concerns.

In 2013, under the new MAP-21 law (Moving Ahead for Progress in the 21st Century Act,
effective October 2, 2012), the FTA notified the TOC that it did NOT meet certification
requirements, based on nine specific deficiencies including: the TOC is only a committee created
by MOU with no enabling legislation; it cannot hire its own staff or promulgate/enforce
regulations; its members are managed by their home jurisdictions (not by the TOC itself) and can
be reassigned to other duties; its chair position rotates every 2 years between jurisdictions,
disrupting continuity; and all three jurisdictions have their own separate funding and political
relationships with WMATA that can create conflicts of interest.

At the time of this letter, the TOC had only THREE full-time employees, each paid by and
accountable to a different jurisdiction. The TOC had no offices, had never conducted a single
independent investigation into any WMATA accident or incident (all investigations were
delegated back to WMATA itself), and had no enforcement authority.

COMPARISON TO FRA OVERSIGHT (PATH AS PRECEDENT)
The letter contrasts WMATA's oversight regime with the Federal Railroad Administration's (FRA)
oversight of the Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corporation (PATH), a similarly complex rapid
transit system (13.8 miles, about half underground, 244,000 daily weekday passengers, 10 speed
limits from 8-55 mph). PATH is classified as a "commuter authority" under 45 USC Section
1104(3) and is therefore subject to full FRA regulatory oversight, including civil penalties,
individual liability penalties, compliance orders, and emergency orders -- tools the TOC and FTA
do not have. The FRA employs about 400 federal safety inspectors plus about 165 state
inspectors (Maryland and Virginia both participate in the FRA's State Inspection Program, with 3
and 6 FRA-qualified inspectors respectively).

THE AUGUST 6, 2015 DERAILMENT -- ILLUSTRATING THE OVERSIGHT GAP
On August 6, 2015, WMATA Metrorail train 412 (a nonrevenue employee train) derailed
approaching the Smithsonian interlocking; three of six cars derailed. Investigation found that a
July 9, 2015 track inspection had identified a "wide gage" defect (58.09 inches, exceeding
WMATA's 57.75-inch maximum standard) at the exact location of the later derailment. WMATA
policy required immediately removing that track from service until repairs were completed --
but the defect remained in the track for 27 days while WMATA continued running revenue
service over it with no speed reduction or other mitigation, until the derailment occurred. Under
FRA oversight, a wide-gage defect like this would have mandated removing the track from
service, with continued operation permitted only at a maximum 15 mph for no more than 30 days
under a designated person's authorization.

CONCLUSION AND URGENT RECOMMENDATIONS
The NTSB noted it had initiated 11 investigations of the WMATA rail system over the prior 33
years, resulting in 18 fatalities total, and concluded that "the TOC cannot perform effective safety
oversight of the WMATA rail system," and further that neither FTA's MAP-21 regulatory changes
nor a proposed new "Metro Safety Commission" would likely resolve the identified deficiencies.

The NTSB made two URGENT safety recommendations to the US Department of Transportation:
(R-15-31): Seek an amendment to 45 USC Section 1104(3) to list WMATA as a "commuter
authority," thus authorizing the FRA to exercise regulatory oversight of WMATA's rail system.
(R-15-32): After that amendment, direct the FRA Administrator to develop and implement a plan
to transition oversight of WMATA's rail system to the FRA within 6 months.

ADDITIONAL DETAIL: RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FIXED-GUIDEWAY OVERSIGHT GAPS
The oversight weaknesses documented in this letter regarding WMATA's Tri-State Oversight
Committee are structurally similar to, but legally distinct from, the oversight gap documented in
the 2011 Miami International Airport automated people mover accident report elsewhere in this
corpus. In the WMATA case, a State Safety Oversight Agency (the TOC) existed under 49 CFR
Part 659 but lacked meaningful enforcement authority. In the Miami APM case, no oversight
regime applied at all, because the system predated Florida's 1984 oversight statute and received
no FTA funding, placing it outside 49 CFR Part 659's scope entirely. Both cases were cited by
the NTSB as evidence that the pre-MAP-21 (and, in WMATA's case, even the initial post-MAP-21)
oversight framework for U.S. rail fixed-guideway systems left significant safety gaps, whether
through weak enforcement of existing rules (WMATA) or the complete absence of any
applicable rule (Miami APM). This letter's proposed remedy -- placing WMATA under Federal
Railroad Administration (FRA) jurisdiction as a "commuter authority" -- was a WMATA-specific
fix and would not, by itself, have addressed the broader class of non-FTA-funded fixed-guideway
systems (airport people movers, amusement park monorails and inclines, etc.) identified as an
oversight gap in the Miami APM report's Appendix B, which listed at least 22 states with such
unregulated systems as of 2011.
