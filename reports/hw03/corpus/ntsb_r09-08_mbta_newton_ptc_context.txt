WHAT HAPPENED (per the O'Hare report's citation)
On May 28, 2008, at 5:51 p.m. eastern daylight time, Massachusetts Bay Transportation
Authority (MBTA) Green Line train 3667 was traveling westbound at about 38 mph and struck
the rear of westbound Green Line train 3681, which had stopped for a red signal, in a suburb of
Boston. The operator of the striking train was killed; one passenger was seriously injured; four
passengers and three crewmembers sustained minor injuries.

THE RESULTING RECOMMENDATION AND ITS LONG, UNRESOLVED HISTORY
On July 23, 2009, as a direct result of this accident, the NTSB recommended that the FTA
"facilitate the development and implementation of positive train control systems for rail transit
systems nationwide" (Safety Recommendation R-09-08). The FTA's initial response was to
organize a November 2009 meeting with transit train control experts; by June 2010 FTA said it
was developing a research proposal. The NTSB classified the recommendation "Open-Acceptable
Response" at that point -- but received no further substantive updates from FTA over the
following five years, despite the NTSB's general expectation that recommendation responses be
completed within five years. During that same period, the NTSB continued investigating multiple
other rail transit accidents (including the 2014 O'Hare collision) where a Positive Train Control
(PTC) or Transmission-Based Train Control (TBTC) system would likely have prevented the
accident. By 2015, once MAP-21 had given FTA clearer legislative authority to actually require
such systems, the NTSB reclassified the original 2009 recommendation R-09-08 as
"Closed-Unacceptable Action/Superseded," replacing it with a new, more specific 2015
recommendation (R-15-22) requiring rail transit agencies to implement TBTC systems that
prevent train collisions.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR CROSS-DOCUMENT SYNTHESIS
This six-year gap between the 2009 recommendation and its 2015 supersession -- spanning at
least two other fatal or serious WMATA-unrelated transit accidents referenced elsewhere in this
corpus (the 2009 Fort Totten collision, which is a separate WMATA-specific matter, and this
2014 CTA O'Hare accident) -- illustrates the same recurring pattern of slow-moving federal
oversight documented in the WMATA-specific recommendation history file in this corpus
(ntsb_recommendations_status_wmata_history.txt), but shows that this is not a WMATA-only
problem: it reflects a broader, system-wide weakness in FTA's pre-MAP-21 statutory authority
across multiple, unrelated transit agencies (WMATA in Washington D.C., MBTA in Boston, and
CTA in Chicago) in different states, all citing the same underlying legal gap.
