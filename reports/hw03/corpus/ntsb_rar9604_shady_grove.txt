NTSB/RAR-96/04
Collision of Washington Metropolitan Area Transit Authority Train T-111 with Standing
Train at Shady Grove Passenger Station, Gaithersburg, Maryland, January 6, 1996
Source: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/rar9604.pdf
Accessed: 2026-09-19

ABSTRACT
On January 6, 1996, WMATA Metrorail subway train No. T-111 failed to come to a stop at the
above-ground Shady Grove, Maryland, passenger station, the final station on the Metrorail Red
Line. The four-car train ran by the station platform and continued about 470 feet into the
Metrorail yard north of the station, where it struck a standing, unoccupied "gap" subway train
that was awaiting assignment. The operator of train T-111 was fatally injured; the train's two
passengers were not injured. Total property damages were estimated to be between $2.1 and $2.6
million.

PROBABLE CAUSE
The Safety Board determined the probable cause was the failure of WMATA management and
board of directors (1) to fully understand and address the design features and incompatibilities of
the automatic train control (ATC) system before establishing automatic train operation as the
standard operating mode at all times and in all weather conditions, (2) to permit Operations
Control Center (OCC) controllers and supervisors to use their own experience, knowledge, and
judgment to make decisions involving safety, and (3) to effectively promulgate and enforce a
prohibition against placing standby ("gap") trains at terminal stations on the same track as
incoming trains. Contributing to the severity of the operator's injuries was the disproportionate
crush sustained by the lead cars of the colliding trains.

ACCIDENT NARRATIVE
A winter storm warning was in effect for the Washington, D.C. area on the night of the accident,
with snow beginning around 9:10 p.m. Multiple trains began overrunning station platforms
because of slippery tracks. The OCC button controller stated that between 10:00 and 10:30 p.m.,
he asked the OCC superintendent on several occasions for permission to allow train operators to
switch to manual mode, and was denied each time -- consistent with a WMATA policy (issued
November 17, 1995) prohibiting mode changes except in emergencies.

At 10:27:35 p.m., train T-111 overran the Twinbrook station by all four cars while under
performance level 3 (44 mph limit); it was instructed to skip servicing that station and continue in
automatic mode. At 10:31:55 p.m., it overran the Rockville station platform by one car. Because
its lead car was outside the station's platform limits when the train finished servicing Rockville,
the previously assigned performance level (a 59-mph limit) was lost, and the train's speed control
system defaulted to performance level 1 -- a 75-mph limit -- for the next segment toward Shady
Grove. At 10:35:30 p.m., the operator reported console speed readouts of "75 limiting and 75
regulated," meaning the train was authorized to reach 75 mph on a segment where it should have
been limited to 59. The OCC radio controller told him to continue in automatic mode. At
10:36:40, the operator reported his actual speed had reached 75 mph; the controller acknowledged
but did not intervene. Approaching the Shady Grove station, the operator reported his speed had
dropped to "35/35" -- but syslog data show the train was still traveling in excess of 50 mph and
about 1,140 feet from the platform at that moment. The train never actually slowed to the
expected stopping profile: it struck the gap train at a calculated speed of 22-29 mph, roughly 470
feet past the end of the platform.

The Red Line radio controller told investigators: "At this time I had a feeling the system was
doing what it was supposed to do... I didn't feel I had an emergency where I could step in and
overrule -- put my job on the line -- and tell the man to go manual."

TRAIN OPERATOR AND MEDICAL FINDINGS
The 48-year-old operator had been promoted from bus operator to train operator on October 29,
1995 -- only about 10 weeks before the accident -- and had operated on the Red Line for roughly
30 duty days total. Postmortem toxicology found therapeutic levels of acetaminophen and codeine
(from Tylenol 3, which he had a documented history of taking for chronic back pain) plus several
over-the-counter cold-medication ingredients; no evidence indicated these medications
contributed to the accident, and the report treated the medications as a background factor rather
than a cause.

WHEEL FLATS AND THE DECISION TO ELIMINATE MANUAL OPERATION
An unusually high number of "wheel flat" incidents occurred November 11-14, 1995 (77 flat-
wheel events, 47% of that entire month's total, coinciding with 2.47 inches of precipitation -- more
than half of November's total rainfall). A November 15, 1995 WMATA Vehicle Engineering
Branch memo concluded the flats were "operator-induced" because the cars were being operated
in manual mode at the time, and recommended curtailing manual operation during winter storms
in favor of standardizing automatic (ATC) operation. The director of engineering and operations
support who received this memo said he considered more testing was needed before endorsing
the conclusion, and did not forward the recommendation to top management -- yet the policy was
implemented anyway on November 17, 1995, apparently based on separate verbal guidance from
the general superintendent for rail transportation. This is a key finding: the actual policy change
that set up the accident was NOT formally traceable to the engineering analysis that supposedly
justified it.

Later testing (December 1995-January 1996) found that the Breda 2000/3000-series cars'
slip/slide braking system, under wet-rail conditions, achieved average deceleration rates as low as
1.24 mph/sec in simulated tests -- well below the 1.65-2.2 mph/sec range the ATC block signaling
system's design assumed was available. This meant the automatic system, now mandatory in all
weather, could authorize speeds assuming braking performance the cars could not actually deliver
under low-adhesion conditions.

EMERGENCY RESPONSE FINDINGS
The OCC's initial 911 call did not include the status of third-rail power or the best access points,
as required by the Metrorail Safety Rules and Procedures Handbook (MSRPH) Section 5.1A.25.
A dispatcher used the wrong chain marker, causing some firefighters to respond to the Shady
Grove station itself rather than the accident site roughly 500 feet beyond it. A section of third rail
remained energized for 43 minutes after the accident because an OCC controller was unable to
remotely trip circuit breaker "33" from the command console (attempts at 10:41, 10:42, and 11:22
p.m. failed); station personnel eventually operated a manual emergency trip switch at 11:23 p.m.
Only one Warning Strobe and Alarm Device (WSAD, which visually/audibly indicates if power is
restored) reached the scene initially, out of a recommended two. No third-rail schematics were
posted at the accident location to help firefighters understand which rail sections were controlled
from where.

Two uninjured passengers aboard the striking train were left waiting 15-30 minutes in the dark,
damaged train before rescuers made contact, because responders searching for casualties did not
realize passengers remained aboard and prioritized establishing that the third rail was de-energized
before allowing their egress.

REGULATORY CONTEXT
WMATA was a member of the American Public Transit Association (APTA) and received
periodic APTA safety audits (most recently December 1993, with a June 1995 follow-up); the
audit summary stated "most of the essential elements of the WMATA System Safety Program
Plan were effectively implemented" -- a conclusion this accident directly contradicted regarding
management decision-making and communication. In response to the Intermodal Surface
Transportation Efficiency Act of 1991, the FTA's December 27, 1995 final rule (49 CFR Part 659,
effective January 26, 1996) required states with non-FRA-regulated rail fixed-guideway systems
to designate a state safety oversight agency. WMATA proposed a new Tri-State Oversight
Committee (TOC) with six members (two each from Virginia, Maryland, and D.C.) in response --
the same body that, 13 years later, was found by the 2010 Fort Totten investigation to have
provided ineffective oversight, showing a recurring institutional weakness across multiple WMATA
accidents over more than a decade.
